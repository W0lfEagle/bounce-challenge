# Bounce and the future of access to things

Business, technology and social impact report | 10 October 2026

Prepared for interview discussion. Independent analysis based on public sources and the vision described in the conversation. Recommendations, financial examples and pilot targets are proposals, not Bounce plans or operating results.

## Executive assessment

The attractive idea is that access to a useful object becomes more convenient than keeping, transporting or buying one. A traveller arrives without a buggy; a suitable one is waiting at the hotel. A household stores seasonal belongings and requests them back when needed. A neighbour completes a repair using a shared tool rather than buying it.

These experiences have different business models. Returning **my particular item** requires storage, inventory accuracy and transport. Providing **an equivalent item nearby** requires rental supply, suitability matching and dependable availability. Combining them in one account makes sense; treating them as one interchangeable service does not.

Bounce has publicly described a vision of sending stored belongings to wherever customers need them. Its current website advertises luggage storage and delivery; Japan delivery is offered through Yamato Transport. The broader shared rental proposition in this report comes from the interview context and should not be presented as an announced Bounce product. [Bounce's public vision](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/bounce_happy-new-year-2024-was-a-year-full-activity-7280610259801427968-J6Zo), [Bounce delivery in Japan](https://bounce.com/luggage-delivery/japan).

**Recommendation:** test a managed local rental service in a tightly defined travel destination, using qualified local suppliers, selected Bounce handoff points and scheduled delivery. Keep personal storage retrieval as a separate product track. Add household peer suppliers only when inspection, custody and owner access rules work reliably.

The closest competitors are BabyQuip and Babonbo for delivered baby equipment, Hygglo for peer rental, and Clutter and LOVESPACE for stored belongings returned on request. Tool libraries and libraries of things demonstrate affordable community access and repair; they also show that inventory stewardship and local relationships matter as much as discovery software.

The likely advantage is **reliable access through a dense, trusted local network**, rather than the novelty of renting objects. The critical unknowns are delivery cost per completed order, eligible supply density, willingness to pay for an arrival guarantee, and whether the service reduces purchases or merely adds consumption.

## 1. What exactly is being shared?

| Service | Customer promise | Main economic constraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personal storage and retrieval | Return my exact item, in its recorded condition | Storage space, retrieval labour and transport |
| Peer rental at destination | Supply an agreed item owned by someone nearby | Owner availability, suitability, inspection and handoffs |
| Managed shared inventory | Supply a guaranteed item from a maintained pool | Capital, depreciation, maintenance and utilisation |
| Cooperative or community library | Affordable access to a locally governed collection | Funding, opening hours, staff and volunteer capacity |

**Shared access is not necessarily shared ownership.** An owner renting a buggy keeps title to it. A cooperative can own a pool collectively. A platform-owned fleet provides access without giving users equity or governance. Bounce should choose its legal and operating model explicitly before describing the service as shared ownership.

There is a promising bridge: customers can opt to make selected stored belongings rentable. But it introduces competing rights. If an owner asks for a buggy currently on loan, Bounce cannot promise immediate return. Contracts need blocked dates, minimum notice, permitted uses, wear allowances and the owner's ability to withdraw an item after outstanding bookings. Renting must be opt-in for each item, never the default for personal storage.

For international travel, the sensible default is local substitution: reserve suitable equipment at the destination. That avoids transporting the same object across a border, but does not remove destination safety, payments or tax obligations. Shipping a customer's exact item internationally is a separate, slower service requiring route-specific customs and carrier assessment.

Substitution must be explicit. A buggy has requirements such as child weight, reclining position, terrain suitability, folded dimensions and included accessories. “A buggy” is not a sufficient specification. Personalised or sentimental possessions, and equipment requiring individual fitting, are much less substitutable.

### What tool sheds and thing libraries teach

A neighbourhood tool shed makes sense when rarely used equipment is costly to purchase, needs maintenance, and can be collected nearby. A library of things broadens this to cleaners, sewing machines, camping equipment and other occasional-use objects. A curated collection offers known condition and predictable specifications; a peer marketplace offers more variety with greater variability.

Men's Sheds are a related social model rather than a direct rental competitor. Their association describes shared practical activities, making and mending, skills and friendship. Borrowing equipment and participating in a workshop are different services: delivery can improve access to objects, but it does not reproduce the relationships created by working together. [UK Men's Sheds Association](https://menssheds.org.uk/what-is-a-mens-shed/).

The transferable lessons are to choose repairable items, fund maintenance, explain safe use, and provide a human point of help. SHARE Oxford explicitly describes volunteer involvement and financial or object donations. Its prices should therefore not be used as evidence that a commercial delivered service can operate at the same cost. [SHARE Oxford's operating model](https://shareoxford.org/).

## 3. Business model and competitive position

### Start with a specific customer problem

The best initial hypothesis is a family staying several days in a dense destination who values a clean, suitable buggy available before arrival. That need has a deadline, a known address and a clear alternative: bring equipment, rent elsewhere, borrow from the hotel, or purchase locally and potentially resell. Compare the full cost and inconvenience of those alternatives, including airline policies and second-hand options, rather than assuming every rental displaces a new purchase.

Other segments behave differently. Residents borrowing a drill may favour low-cost collection. Seasonal storage users may accept several days' notice. A photographer may demand a specific model. Avoid launching these categories together; each adds different inspection, insurance, support and inventory requirements.

### Choose how much operational control to buy

| Model | Strength | Main tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open peer marketplace | Broad supply without buying a fleet | Uncertain acceptance, condition and calendars |
| Qualified supplier marketplace | Local expertise, maintained stock and repeatable service | Suppliers require attractive payouts and can sell elsewhere |
| Bounce-owned fleet | Control over availability and standards | Capital exposure, storage and idle seasonal inventory |
| Cooperative or community partnership | Local accountability and affordable access | Governance, funding and shared service obligations |

**Recommended starting model:** qualified local suppliers, inspected equipment, and a small contracted backup pool. Selected Bounce partners act as handoff points where appropriate. Ordinary luggage partners should qualify separately before handling rental inspection or cleaning. Over time, private owners could consign approved equipment into professionally managed pools.

Revenue can include rental commission, clearly priced delivery, storage subscriptions for personal belongings, and hotel or property partnerships. A household membership is worth testing only once repeat use is observed. Unlimited delivery promises are dangerous when each retrieval consumes paid labour. International access should initially mean one account and local fulfilment, not reciprocal free shipping.

Bounce's website advertises 32,000 storage locations across 4,000 cities. This is a company claim and a distribution starting point, not proof of rental-ready capacity. The relevant measure is qualified, available stock and delivery capacity within each service area. [Bounce's advertised network](https://bounce.com/?C=5745).

Defensibility would come from dependable local supply, hospitality distribution, custody records, safety operations and increasingly efficient routes. Network effects are local and category-specific: more drills in London do little for a buggy request in Lisbon. Community partnerships need agreed access and revenue terms, particularly if tourist demand could crowd out residents.

Size the first market from local evidence: eligible arriving families multiplied by the proportion needing equipment, observed paid conversion and average booking value. Then cap fulfilled demand by compatible item-days and route capacity during peak dates. Extend this calculation across validated districts instead of combining the whole travel, storage and rental markets into one optimistic headline.

## 4. Economics: convenience must pay for both journeys

**Illustrative assumptions only.** The following example is a modelling exercise in GBP, with tax excluded for comparability. It is not a competitor quotation or a forecast. Real consumer prices must include applicable taxes and disclose mandatory fees.

Assume a five-day rental at £10 per day, plus £20 for delivery and collection. Customer payment is £70. Bounce retains 25% of the £50 rental charge and the delivery charge, paying £37.50 to the supplier. This example assumes the supplier funds routine item depreciation and cleaning from that payout; Bounce funds transport and the listed platform costs.

| Per completed order | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Customer payment | £70.00 |
| Supplier payout | £37.50 |
| Platform retained amount before variable costs | £32.50 |
| Payment cost: assumed 3% of payment + £0.30 | £2.40 |
| Partner handoff fees | £3.00 |
| Variable customer support | £2.00 |
| Assumed insurance premium and uninsured loss allowance | £2.00 |
| Remaining amount before transport | £23.10 |

| Combined delivery and collection cost | Contribution before acquisition and fixed costs |
| --- | ---: |
| £12, efficient batched routes | £11.10 |
| £20, moderate route efficiency | £3.10 |
| £30, sparse or disrupted routes | -£6.90 |

The £23.10 figure is the maximum transport spend before this example becomes contribution-negative. At the £20 scenario, even £8 of acquisition cost makes the first order lose £4.90 before fixed overhead. Treat hotel referrals and repeat demand as hypotheses to measure, not free acquisition.

**Supplier economics need their own test.** If a supplier receives £37.50, incurs £6 cleaning, £4 handling and £5 wear or maintenance allowance, £22.50 remains before acquisition of the item, storage, overhead and taxes. If acquisition cost less eventual resale value is £250, roughly 12 such rentals recover that capital under these assumptions. Downtime, cancellations and major repairs extend payback.

With the same assumptions, a one-day £10 rental plus £20 transport charge retains £22.50 before variable costs. After £1.20 payment cost, £7 other listed costs and £20 transport, contribution is -£5.70. Multi-day bookings, bundles, collection points and batching are therefore plausible routes to viability; actual customer willingness to pay still needs testing.

Track gross booking value, supplier payouts, recognised revenue and contribution separately. Principal-versus-agent accounting depends on contractual control and needs review. Do not call retained cash revenue automatically. Avoid a total addressable market headline until local demand and capacity can be estimated from evidence.

For personal storage, use a separate model: subscription revenue minus space, inbound handling, insurance, support and paid retrieval. Charging by occupied volume while offering free unlimited retrieval can create a large cost mismatch. Record retrieval frequency, labour minutes and route cost per household before setting a subscription.

## 5. Physical operations and trust

The operational system needs a named custodian at every stage: owner or supplier, inspection point, carrier, delivery venue, borrower, return carrier and inspection point again. A shared record should explain who had the item, when, and in what condition. The product promise is only as strong as the weakest handoff.

Before listing equipment, record make, model, serial number where present, ownership evidence, condition, accessories, instructions and relevant safety checks. For buggies, inspect the frame, brakes, harness, folding locks and manufacturer limits; retain cleaning and maintenance history. Specialist safety procedures and the insurer's requirements should define the actual inspection checklist.

At checkout, show the exact model or agreed substitution class, complete price, dates, delivery window, included accessories and return process. Use request-to-book where suppliers must confirm availability. Offer instant confirmation only against reliable stock and capacity. Obtain hotel consent before unattended reception delivery; record the recipient and approved collection arrangements.

Use scans and photographs at intake, dispatch, handoff and return. Photographs support condition assessment but cannot prove every functional safety characteristic. A returned item is unavailable until inspected, cleaned and released. Quarantine damaged, incomplete or recalled items immediately.

Prepare for flight delays, closed venues, absent owners, courier cancellations, late returns and missing accessories. Define a replacement hierarchy, escalation owner, cancellation terms, refunds and compensation before selling an arrival guarantee. An emergency replacement can cost more than the order's entire margin.

Insurance needs to map onto custody: stored property, transit, theft, accidental damage, product liability and third-party injury are different risks. A headline luggage protection limit does not establish cover for rented equipment. Identify deductibles, exclusions, country scope, claim evidence and payout timing; obtain category-specific quotes.

Begin with scheduled windows and explicit service areas. Batch outbound and return journeys where possible and reserve contingency capacity. Reserve costly on-demand service for customers willing to pay for it. The strongest early operational design may use hotel reception or nearby hubs to aggregate several orders.

## 6. Social and environmental impact

### Benefits that deserve testing

Shared access can reduce upfront spending, household clutter and the burden of transporting equipment. Owners and local businesses may receive income; repair and inspection can create skilled work. Tool access can support home repairs and practical learning. These outcomes are strongest when people can use the service affordably and when items stay useful for longer.

Library of Things' 2024/25 report page states that 48.8% of respondents said they now buy fewer things generally and are more likely to rent or buy second-hand. This is self-reported evidence from that service, with the sample and methodology not established on the accessible landing page. It supports further investigation; it does not prove Bounce would achieve the same outcome. [Library of Things impact report](https://start.libraryofthings.co.uk/2025-london-impact-report).

Library of Things also describes a 25% concession discount at its Barbican service, community partnerships and maintenance by a repair technician. These are concrete mechanisms Bounce could learn from: affordable rates, community venues and repair capacity. [Barbican launch](https://www.libraryofthings.co.uk/blog/library-of-things-opens-at-the-barbican-library).

### Ways the model could fail socially

Deposits, card holds and smartphone-only access can exclude households with limited cash or connectivity. Paid transport can turn inexpensive borrowing into an expensive convenience service. The owners of desirable stock may gain income while low-income users face fees. Tourist peaks could reduce resident access. Courier earnings can look attractive until waiting, returns, mileage and insurance are included.

Mitigations include a low-cost collection option, assisted booking through community partners, transparent owner payouts, a fairly priced damage policy and measurement of workers' earnings after expenses. A community pilot could protect a resident allocation and fund concession borrowing through a disclosed sponsorship or partnership budget. These are proposed choices, not self-financing benefits.

If collective ownership is important, explore a cooperative pool with member voting, published allocation rules and shared surplus. That has different governance and capital needs from a platform that takes commission on privately owned stock. Local partners should have a role in deciding which items to stock and what happens when supply is scarce.

### Avoid assuming rental is automatically greener

A life-cycle study of electric tool rental identifies transportation as an important environmental constraint; research on peer product sharing also considers manufacturing, logistics, use, infrastructure and avoided purchases. The findings are category-specific and do not establish a carbon saving for buggy rental. [Electric tool rental study, abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959652620352896), [Peer product sharing assessment](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/11/1/190).

A useful accounting framework is: avoided production attributable to displaced purchases, less additional shared stock, transport, cleaning, maintenance and infrastructure impacts. Use a consistent life-cycle boundary to avoid counting manufacturing twice. Renting instead of bringing an already-owned buggy may improve convenience without avoiding manufacture. Financial savings can also be spent on other carbon-intensive activities.

| Outcome | Measurement approach |
| --- | --- |
| Affordable access | Total spend versus the user's realistic alternative; concession use; failures due to deposits or cards |
| Purchases avoided | Ask what would otherwise have happened, then follow up on a sample; distinguish bringing an owned item from buying |
| Local livelihoods | Supplier earnings and courier pay after time and expenses; geographic distribution |
| Longer useful life | Repair events, completed rental cycles, retirement causes and verified reuse at disposal |
| Environmental effect | Actual route distance, transport mode, cleaning inputs and category-specific production factors; report uncertainty |
| Community benefit | Resident availability, partner feedback and skills participation; measure relationships separately from transactions |

Do not advertise one purchase or a fixed carbon saving avoided per loan. Publish assumptions and distinguish observed outcomes from modelled estimates.

## 7. Technological considerations

### The core is a reservation and custody system

**Proposed architecture:** start with a modular application, a transactional relational database, object storage for evidence, a queue for background work and adapters for payments and logistics. A small team does not need distributed microservices to validate a city pilot. The hard problem is making inventory, money and real-world handoffs agree.

The following records should be distinct:

- **Product specification:** category, model, suitability attributes, accessories and approved substitution rules.
- **Physical asset:** unique item ID, owner, current custodian and location, serial number, condition and safety status.
- **Availability:** owner blocks, reservations, maintenance time, cleaning buffers and dispatch or return capacity.
- **Booking:** requested period, agreed specifications, delivery and collection windows, prices and policy versions.
- **Custody event:** asset, handing-over party, receiving party, time, evidence and scan source.
- **Money and claims:** authorisations, charges, refunds, supplier obligations, payouts, deposits and disputes.

An illustrative flow is **available -> held -> reserved -> dispatched -> on loan -> returned awaiting inspection -> available**. Quarantine is a separate blocking condition and must be able to interrupt fulfilment. Cancellation, failed delivery and late return require explicit recovery paths. Asset status alone is insufficient: future reservations and safety blocks must also be checked.

### Correct availability before clever matching

Prevent overlapping bookings at database level, using transactional allocation or equivalent enforced constraints. Include cleaning and transport buffers in occupied intervals. For interchangeable stock, atomically reserve category capacity, then assign an eligible physical item before dispatch. A late return must trigger review of downstream bookings; an extension cannot silently consume another customer's reservation.

Store event timestamps consistently, while displaying booking windows in the destination's local time zone. Preserve the intended local date and zone so daylight-saving changes do not move a promised handoff. Use expiring checkout holds. Revalidate asset eligibility and transport capacity when a booking is confirmed and again before dispatch.

Rank only inventory that meets hard constraints: location, dates, age or weight suitability, condition, accessories and delivery capability. Then rank by total price, reliability and preferences. A language model may help structure an owner's listing or translate instructions, but must not infer safety approval or make an undocumented substitution. Deterministic eligibility checks remain authoritative.

### Reliable money and logistics

Use a marketplace payment provider for tokenised checkout, provider verification and supported payouts. Separate rental charges from refundable deposits or temporary authorisations. Holds have provider-specific expiry limits; a five-day rental plus advance booking and inspection can outlast an initial authorisation. The payment policy needs to match the entire booking timeline.

Give charges, refunds, payout requests and courier bookings idempotency keys. Persist intent before external calls; handle signed webhooks, duplicates, delays and out-of-order events. Use durable background jobs or an outbox to avoid losing work between committing a booking and notifying a carrier. A timeout is an uncertain outcome until reconciled, not proof that a payment or courier booking failed.

Do not promise technical “exactly once” across independent external systems. Reconcile provider records against the internal ledger, expose uncertain states to operators and provide compensating actions, such as cancelling a carrier after a booking is refunded. Schedule supplier payout around agreed handoff and dispute rules; do not retain funds indefinitely without a defined policy.

### Make physical exceptions visible

A partner application should scan labels, record condition, display only assigned tasks and queue events during connectivity loss. Offline scans require unique event IDs and conflict review; physical possession may have changed before the server sees it. A static QR label identifies an item but should not authorise collection. Verify the recipient with a booking-specific code or other controlled proof.

An operations console needs overdue returns, expiring inspections, unresolved payment states, failed delivery attempts, missing scans and affected future bookings. Keep the history of corrections rather than silently overwriting custody records. Integrate recall sources and manufacturer notices; test a drill that blocks every affected asset and contacts current borrowers.

Collect only the identity and suitability data needed. Verification can normally return a status or token rather than putting raw identity documents in the application database. Restrict home addresses and travel details to the people who need them; apply encryption, role-based access, retention policies and audit logs. Assess third-party tracking and cross-border data processing. [ICO data minimisation guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-protection-principles/a-guide-to-the-data-protection-principles/data-minimisation/?q=privacy).

### Build, buy and verify

Buy commodity capabilities such as payments, verification, notifications and courier access. Evaluate existing lending software before building the inventory administration layer: myTurn offers reservations, check-in/out, barcode scanning, maintenance reminders, multiple locations and locker integrations. Check APIs, exports, data portability and marketplace payout fit before adopting it. [myTurn features](https://myturn.com/).

Build the differentiated eligibility, service promise, custody workflow and operational recovery. Start with QR or barcode labels; use RFID or connected lockers only when handling volume and measured error rates justify the cost. Route optimisation and demand prediction become useful after real pickup, return and seasonal data exist.

Verification should exercise simultaneous bookings for the final item, abandoned holds, late returns, recalled stock, daylight-saving boundaries, duplicate webhooks, payment timeouts, failed courier bookings, offline scan conflicts and wrong-recipient collection. Monitor successful fulfilment and missing custody evidence, not just API uptime. Test backup restoration and manual recovery before the pilot.

## 8. Legal and commercial design requirements

This section identifies questions to resolve before launch; it does not establish a legal classification for a proposed Bounce product.

**Product safety and liability.** UK guidance identifies responsibilities throughout the supply chain; local trading standards guidance explicitly discusses goods on hire. EU general product safety rules include marketplace requirements, and Northern Ireland differs from Great Britain. Establish the platform's role, applicable category standards, traceability, inspection duties and recall process in each market. [UK product safety guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/product-safety-advice-for-businesses), [Trading standards guidance on hiring goods](https://www.bromley.gov.uk/leaflet/122527/17/683/d), [European Commission product safety](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/business-and-industry/product-safety_en).

**Consumer contracts.** Clarify whether the renter contracts with Bounce, a professional supplier or a private owner; who controls substitutions; and who owes a refund when fulfilment fails. Have counsel review cancellation, deposits, damage charges, subscriptions and consumer disclosures by jurisdiction. A booking for storage is not consent for someone else to rent the stored object.

**Tax, payments and owner income.** Determine VAT or sales-tax treatment, invoicing, seller status, payout verification and reporting obligations. UK platform reporting rules cover specified activities, so confirm how equipment rental, storage and delivery fit instead of assuming all object rental is reportable. Income reporting does not itself determine tax owed. [HMRC platform operator guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/reporting-sellers-to-hmrc-if-youre-a-digital-platform-operator), [HMRC seller guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/selling-goods-or-services-on-a-digital-platform).

**Long-term access subscriptions.** UK government guidance flags hiring or leasing goods for more than three months as an area requiring a consumer credit authorisation check. Evaluate the perimeter and exemptions before offering long-term rental or rent-to-own; a short travel hire and a continuing credit arrangement are different products. [UK consumer credit guidance](https://www.gov.uk/offering-credit-consumers-law).

**Partners and borders.** Define custody, service levels, training, incident reporting, worker arrangements, premises permission and insurer-approved practices. For physical international shipping, assess customs, temporary admission where relevant, prohibited items and who acts as importer. Local destination sourcing reduces shipping complexity while retaining local compliance duties.

## 9. Recommended pilot and decision gates

**Proposed 12-week pilot, followed by repeat-use tracking.** Select one dense travel district using actual Bounce bookings, hotel willingness to receive equipment, qualified supplier stock, local insurance quotes and route costs. Do not choose a city solely for its global tourist volume. Separately test demand for returning personal stored items, with distinct pricing and accounting.

### Weeks 1-3: discover and design

Interview approximately 15-20 travelling families, 8-10 potential suppliers and 5-8 hotel or handoff partners. Ask about recent behaviour, total alternatives, acceptable equipment, delivery windows, damage concerns and willingness to pay. Show a concrete all-in offer and measure reservation intent; where operationally ready, use clearly refundable reservations rather than relying only on survey enthusiasm.

Obtain insurer input and local legal review, cost a complete round trip, agree partner responsibilities and define inspection, cleaning and replacement procedures. If no viable category cover or safe operating process exists, change the category before accepting rentals. Secure backup capacity and a written recovery process.

### Weeks 4-6: controlled service

Onboard roughly 3-5 qualified suppliers and 3-5 delivery or collection venues. Begin with a narrow set of approved buggies, multi-day rentals, scheduled windows and manual dispatch supported by inventory records. Exclude car seats and medical or individually fitted equipment from this first experiment; they add substantial specialist requirements.

Measure the complete work per order, including unsuccessful attempts, returns, inspection and support. Verify supplier stock physically. Confirm that partners are paid enough to perform the work expected of them. Add privately owned stored items only as a separate, supervised test after the managed service works.

### Weeks 7-12: compare and decide

Aim for about 100 completed rentals across delivery and collection options. Use the early cohort to compare bundles, total prices and fulfilment methods without compromising safety or promised bookings. Continue measuring repeat demand after the pilot: a traveller may not need another buggy in the next month.

| Proposed gate, not an industry benchmark | Decision supported |
| --- | --- |
| At least 95% of accepted bookings fulfilled within the agreed window | Is the service dependable enough for expansion? Record all cancellations and denominator definitions. |
| 100% of dispatched items have current required safety and inspection records | Is stock eligible for use? Any serious incident triggers a pause and investigation. |
| Positive contribution after all variable costs on repeatable routes, with acquisition measured separately | Can delivery work without hidden subsidy? Extend the trial if seasonality or volume is insufficient. |
| Owners and suppliers earn acceptable amounts after costs and remain willing to supply | Is available supply durable? Measure signed future availability, not just stated interest. |
| Clear evidence of willingness to pay or committed partner funding | Is there a sustainable buyer for the promise? Separate commercial and funded community services. |
| Affordable collection and credible purchase-displacement measurement | Can the claimed social or environmental benefit be demonstrated? |

A 100-order pilot can identify operational failure modes and estimate contribution; it cannot establish rare-event safety, annual utilisation, market-wide carbon savings or long-run retention. Expansion requires more exposure, seasonal observations and category-specific assurance.

Scale one category and neighbouring zones before adding a second city. Test a community tool-library partnership as a separate resident service with its own access rules, budget and impact outcomes. If delivery remains uneconomic, prioritise collection hubs or partner-led fulfilment rather than assuming more software will solve it.

## 10. Questions to take into the interview

- Does “summon” mean the customer's exact possession, an equivalent product, or both? Which problem is the next actual product priority?
- What can current partners reliably do beyond storing bags: receive courier deliveries, inspect items, clean them or hold overnight stock?
- Is the intended supply privately owned, professionally operated, Bounce-owned or cooperatively owned? Who carries idle inventory and damage risk?
- What service level is the customer buying, and who supplies the replacement when an owner or carrier fails?
- Which evidence already exists for local rental demand, delivery cost and partner capacity?
- Is social impact a product constraint, a separately funded programme, or an outcome measured after launch?

An interview position worth defending: **Bounce could become the trusted service for accessing physical things across places. The route there is to make a narrow promise reliable, measure the cost of every handoff, and use local shared stock where equivalence is acceptable. Community ownership and affordability should be designed explicitly if they are part of the mission.**
